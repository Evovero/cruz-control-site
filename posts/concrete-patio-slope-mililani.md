<p>A concrete patio in Mililani should slope away from your house by at least <a href="https://www.ohioconcrete.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/GUIDELINES-FOR-DURABLE-CURRENT-6-2016.pdf">1/8 inch per foot</a>, and 1/4 inch per foot gives better protection against standing water. That small tilt moves rain off the surface and away from your foundation. We set it before the pour, because it is very hard to fix afterward.</p>

<h2>Why Slope Matters on a Mililani Patio</h2>
<p>A patio that looks flat is rarely perfectly flat, and it should not be. Water that sits on a slab can stain it, grow algae on it, and make it slippery. Water that runs toward the house can end up at the foundation or under a door threshold. A patio is a drainage surface as much as it is an outdoor room.</p>
<p>Many Mililani lots have a patio that backs onto a yard with its own grade. Over our 12+ years of flatwork on Oahu, we have seen the same pattern again and again: the pour is easy, and the planning of where the water goes is what decides whether the patio ages well. Our team walks the lot before we quote, looking at where the roof drains, where the yard falls and where a patio can safely shed water.</p>

<h2>How Much Slope Is Enough?</h2>
<p>The <a href="https://www.ohioconcrete.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/GUIDELINES-FOR-DURABLE-CURRENT-6-2016.pdf">Ohio Ready Mixed Concrete Association guide</a> for residential flatwork says the finished surface should slope at least 1/8 inch per foot, and that a slope of 1/4 inch per foot provides better assurance that the surface will not pond water. For a 12 foot deep patio, a 1/4 inch per foot slope drops 3 inches from the house to the outer edge.</p>
<p>That is enough to drain, and still gentle enough that tables and chairs sit level to the eye. We like to aim for the steeper end where the layout allows it, and we use the lower number only when the surroundings leave no other choice.</p>

<h3>Which Direction Should the Patio Slope?</h3>
<p>The default answer is away from the house. After that, the question is where the water ends up. A good slab design sends water to lawn, planting beds or a drain that already works, and never onto a neighbor's lot or against a retaining wall. If the yard cannot take the water, the plan needs a catch drain or a change in layout, and we raise that before the pour.</p>

<h3>How Do You Hold a Slope Across a Whole Slab?</h3>
<p>The slope is built into the forms and the base, not carved into the concrete after the pour. We set the form boards to the right fall, compact the base to match and then strike off the concrete against the forms. A slab poured on an uneven base can end up with low spots even if the surface looks right on pour day.</p>

<h2>Slope Is Only Part of the Drainage Plan</h2>
<p>Surface slope works together with the base, the joints and the edges. Each one affects whether water leaves the patio or lingers.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The base:</strong> A compacted, even base keeps the slab from settling into low spots that hold water.</li>
<li><strong>The edges:</strong> Where the patio meets soil, we grade the soil so it falls away and does not trap water against the slab edge.</li>
<li><strong>The house side:</strong> Flashing, siding and door thresholds need clearance, so we plan the finished height before we set forms.</li>
<li><strong>The finish:</strong> A broom finish gives grip when wet. A very smooth trowel finish can be slick, so we choose the texture with drainage in mind.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Joints, Thickness and Cracking</h2>
<p>Drainage and cracking are connected. Water that finds its way into cracks can soften the base under a slab. The same guide recommends a thickness of <a href="https://www.ohioconcrete.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/GUIDELINES-FOR-DURABLE-CURRENT-6-2016.pdf">3.5 to 4 inches</a> for residential flatwork, and says contraction joints should be cut to at least one quarter of the slab thickness. For a 4 inch slab, it suggests spacing joints about 10 feet apart, with panels close to square.</p>
<p>Joints give the concrete a planned place to crack, so the slab does not choose its own. We lay them out before the pour so that they line up with the shape of the patio, the posts and any change in direction. If you plan a larger outdoor space, we also think about how the joint pattern looks from the house.</p>

<h3>Patios Beside Other Structures</h3>
<p>If your patio meets a wall, a lanai post or a slab that already exists, we use an isolation joint at the contact point. That lets the patio and the structure move on their own. It also keeps a crack from running from the corner of one into the other.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>How much slope should a concrete patio have in Mililani?</h3>
<p>Plan for at least 1/8 inch of fall per foot, and aim for 1/4 inch per foot when the layout allows it. The tilt should lead away from the house toward lawn, a bed or a working drain. We confirm the exact fall on site before we set the forms for your patio.</p>
<h3>Will a sloped patio feel uneven?</h3>
<p>Not at 1/4 inch per foot. The tilt is gentle enough that tables and chairs sit steadily, and most people never notice it. Water is what notices it, and it leaves the surface instead of pooling. If you want a level seating area, we can plan one that still drains.</p>
<h3>Can I fix a patio that holds water?</h3>
<p>Sometimes. Minor ponding may be corrected with surface work, but a patio that slopes toward the house or has a settled base often needs to be replaced. We look at the slab, the drainage path and the base before we recommend repair or replacement, so you know which one fits.</p>
<h3>How thick should a concrete patio be?</h3>
<p>The <a href="https://www.ohioconcrete.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/GUIDELINES-FOR-DURABLE-CURRENT-6-2016.pdf">Ohio Ready Mixed Concrete Association guide</a> says 3.5 to 4 inches is generally enough for residential patios, with more only where heavy vehicles will park. We confirm the thickness for your patio based on its use and the ground under it.</p>
<h3>Do I need control joints in my patio?</h3>
<p>Yes. Joints give the slab a planned place to crack, which keeps the surface looking clean. We lay them out so the panels are close to square and the pattern lines up with the patio shape. For a 4 inch slab, the same guide suggests joints about 10 feet apart.</p>
