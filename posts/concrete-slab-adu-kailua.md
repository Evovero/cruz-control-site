<p>A concrete slab for an ADU in Kailua should be planned around three things before anyone sets a form: the city's permit pre-check for the unit, the exact footprint and utility locations from your plans, and a site that drains water away from the new building. Get those settled and the pour itself is the straightforward part. Skip them and the slab can end up in the wrong place, at the wrong height, or waiting on paperwork.</p>

<p>Our team at Cruz Control Concrete Hawaii has spent 12+ years pouring residential slabs across Oahu, from the Waianae Coast to the windward side. Here is how we walk Kailua homeowners through an ADU slab.</p>

<h2>Start With the Permit Pre-Check, Not the Concrete</h2>
<p>An accessory dwelling unit is a full second home on your lot, so it goes through the City and County of Honolulu Department of Planning and Permitting before it is built. The DPP's <a href="https://www.honolulu.gov/dpp/permitting/building-permits-home/building-permits-pre-application/">building permit pre-application page</a> says that before you submit a building permit application for an ADU or ohana unit, you must first get approval through the ADU pre-check form. The same page warns that an application missing required prior approvals will be rejected or suspended until those approvals are in hand.</p>
<p>For a slab, that matters in a simple way: the approved plans decide where the slab goes, how big it is, and what it has to carry. Pouring before the plans are approved is how homeowners end up breaking out new concrete. Work with your designer on the pre-check and plans first, then bring us the approved drawings.</p>

<h2>Match the Slab to the Approved Plans</h2>
<p>An ADU slab is not a patio. It is the floor and foundation for a living space, so it has to match the plans exactly. Before we form anything, we go over:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Footprint and dimensions.</strong> The slab has to line up with the walls, door openings and any porch or landing shown on the plans.</li>
<li><strong>Finished floor height.</strong> The top of the slab sets the floor level for the whole unit, which affects steps, ramps and how water moves around the building.</li>
<li><strong>Thickened edges and footings.</strong> Your plans will show how the edges and any interior bearing points are built. We follow the drawings and the engineer, not a rule of thumb.</li>
<li><strong>Setbacks.</strong> The unit's position on the lot comes from the approved site plan. We stake from it, not from a guess.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Lock In Plumbing and Utilities Before the Pour</h2>
<p>Once concrete is placed, anything underneath it is very hard to change. For an ADU with its own kitchen and bathroom, that means drain lines, water lines and electrical conduit that run under the slab need to be in place, located correctly and inspected before we pour.</p>
<p>We coordinate with your plumber and electrician so their rough-in is done and checked before our crew arrives. A stub-up in the wrong spot under a finished slab is one of the most frustrating and expensive surprises in a small build, and it is entirely avoidable with a walk-through before pour day.</p>

<h2>Prepare the Ground for Windward Conditions</h2>
<p>Kailua is on the windward side of Oahu, and any slab there should be built for wet weather. A slab for a living space needs a base that stays firm when it gets wet and a site that sends water away from the building.</p>
<h3>Base and compaction</h3>
<p>We clear organic material, bring the subgrade to a firm, even condition, and place and compact base material so the slab is supported the same way across its whole area. Soft spots found during excavation are dealt with before the pour, not covered up.</p>
<h3>Moisture under the slab</h3>
<p>Interior floors need protection from moisture coming up from the ground. Your plans will call out what goes under the slab, and we install it as specified so flooring and finishes hold up inside the unit.</p>
<h3>Drainage around the building</h3>
<p>The ground around the new slab should fall away from it so rainwater does not collect against the walls. If the ADU sits near the main house, we look at how roof runoff and yard drainage will move between the two buildings, since a low spot between them is a common problem.</p>

<h2>Curing in Island Heat and Trade Winds</h2>
<p>Sun and wind pull moisture out of fresh concrete quickly, and a slab that dries too fast at the surface can crack and dust. On an ADU slab, where the surface becomes the base for flooring, curing properly is part of the job, not an afterthought. We plan pour timing around the weather, protect the surface from drying out, and keep traffic and framing loads off until the concrete is ready. Our post on <a href="/blog/concrete-curing-kapolei/">curing concrete in sun and wind</a> goes deeper on why this matters on Oahu.</p>

<h2>What to Have Ready Before You Call</h2>
<p>A quick checklist to bring to your first conversation with a concrete contractor:</p>
<ol>
<li>Your approved or in-progress plans, including the site plan and foundation details.</li>
<li>The status of your ADU pre-check and building permit application.</li>
<li>Contact information for your plumber, electrician and designer.</li>
<li>Photos of the build area, including access from the street and any slopes.</li>
<li>Your target timeline for framing, so the slab can be scheduled to fit.</li>
</ol>
<p>Access matters more than people expect. On tight Kailua lots, how we get concrete from the street to the back of the property shapes the plan for pour day, so photos of the path are very helpful.</p>

The plans, inspections and utilities under the slab all carry more weight, so there is less room for improvising on site. <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Do I need a permit to pour a slab for an ADU in Kailua?</h3>
<p>An ADU goes through the City and County of Honolulu Department of Planning and Permitting. Their building permit page says you must get approval through the ADU pre-check before you submit a building permit application for an ADU or ohana. Settle that with your designer before scheduling any concrete work.</p>

<h3>Can you pour the slab before my building permit is approved?</h3>
<p>We do not recommend it. The approved plans decide the slab's location, size, height and the plumbing beneath it. Pouring early risks a slab that does not match what the city approves, which can mean removing and replacing concrete. Waiting for approved plans keeps the job clean and avoids paying for the same slab twice.</p>

<h3>How do you keep moisture out of an ADU floor?</h3>
<p>Moisture protection starts below the slab with a firm, compacted base and whatever under-slab protection your plans specify. Around the building, the ground should slope away so rainwater does not collect at the walls. Together these steps help protect flooring and finishes inside the unit over time.</p>

<h3>What has to happen before the pour?</h3>
<p>Before we pour, the layout is staked from the approved site plan, the base is prepared and compacted, and all under-slab plumbing and electrical rough-in is installed and inspected. We also walk the site with you to confirm finished floor height, drainage and access for pour day.</p>
